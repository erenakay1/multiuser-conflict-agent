| 3 | LLM Agents for Coordinating Multi-User Information Gathering | Harsh Jhamtani, Jacob Andreas, Benjamin Van Durme | 2025 | https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.916/ | Introduces PeopleJoin, a benchmark where an agent gathers information distributed across 2–20 simulated users; popular agent architectures show clear accuracy and efficiency limits. | Supports our use of LLM-simulated users; its observation that too many agent questions burden users motivates our interruption-cost metric. |
| 4 | MAP: Multi-user Personalization with Collaborative LLM-powered Agents | Christine P. Lee, Jihye Choi, Bilge Mutlu | 2025 | https://doi.org/10.1145/3706599.3719853 | A multi-agent workflow (reflection, analysis, feedback) resolves multi-user conflicts better than a single GPT-4 agent; a qualitative study (n=12) shows users value being involved in verifying resolutions. | Closest conflict-resolution work, but uses fixed priority rules and no fairness measure; we compare several strategies and measure fairness numerically. |
| 5 | Collaborative Memory: Multi-User Memory Sharing in LLM Agents with Dynamic Access Control | Alireza Rezazadeh, Zichao Li, Ange Lou, Yuying Zhao, Wei Wei, Yujia Bao | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18279 | A two-tier (private/shared) memory framework with time-varying access graphs enables safe, auditable knowledge sharing across users and agents. | Related multi-user work on privacy and access control; positions privacy as out of our scope. |
| 6 | ConInstruct: Evaluating Large Language Models on Conflict Detection and Resolution in Instructions | Xingwei He, Qianru Zhang, Pengfei Chen, Guanhua Chen, Linlin Yu, Yuan Yuan, Siu-Ming Yiu | 2026 | https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/40356 | Proprietary LLMs and DeepSeek-R1 detect conflicting constraints within instructions with high accuracy, but they rarely inform the user about these conflicts or ask for clarification. Instead, they silently satisfy only part of the constraints, and the constraint that appears later in the prompt tends to win. | This gap between detecting a conflict and communicating it motivates RQ1, since in our setting the conflicts come from equal-authority users rather than from a single prompt. The recency bias is a direct fairness risk for group event planning, because the last user to speak may silently override the others. |
| 7 | Leveraging Large Language Models for Collective Decision-Making | Marios Papachristou, Longqi Yang, Chin-Chia Hsu | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04928v3 | An LLM-based system that collects each member’s preferences through private conversations and refines its proposals over multiple rounds satisfies more members in a more equitable way than single-round baselines. | Its identity-independent evaluation rule offers a formal basis for defining “equal authority,” and its “maximize coverage first, then total satisfaction” rule can serve as a baseline coordination strategy. Its private-channel design can also be compared with a shared group-chat design as one of our strategy conditions. |
| 8 | Multi-User Chat Assistant (MUCA): a Framework Using LLMs to Facilitate Group Conversations | Manqing Mao, Paishun Ting, Yijian Xiang, Mingyang Xu, Julia Chen, Jianzhe Lin | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2401.04883v1 | A modular framework that decides what to say, when to respond, and who to address outperforms a single-prompt baseline in consensus rate, user engagement, and perceived usefulness in group chats. However, it did not improve the evenness of participation. | Its comparison between a single-prompt baseline and a modular controller, and its contrast between imposing an opinion and summarizing all sides, provide a template for comparing coordination strategies among equal-authority users. Its weak evenness result suggests that our evaluation should include a stronger fairness metric for participation. |
